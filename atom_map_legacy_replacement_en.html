Atom Maps: A Governance Replacement Surface for Retiring Legacy Modules with Multi-Agent AI

You asked five AI agents to refactor your 1500-line checkout.py. They returned 30 clean atoms. Are you done? No. The integrated entry point is gone, and nothing currently in your repo stands up and says "I'm the new checkout — retire the old one." That's what atom maps are for.

by Eagl Huang · June 6, 2026 · 6 min read

The price of slicing finely

Atomizing legacy is the easy part. AI agents are great at finding the function boundaries and pulling them out into named, governed units. But four questions remain that no single atom can answer:

  • Which atom is the new entry point? Which atoms play entry-adapter, validator, side-effect-adapter?
  • Where do data-flow, control-flow, and validation edges run between atoms?
  • Does the new constellation actually behave like the old monolith? Which divergences are acceptable?
  • If something goes wrong, is there a safe rollback path back to legacy?

An atom is a cell; a map is an organ. Without a map, even a perfectly shredded legacy module has no single thing that can stand up and say "I'm the new checkout, retire the old one."

The four replacement contracts

A real atom map is more than "a list of members and edges." It promises four things to the outside world — together, they form a governance replacement surface:

  • Structural semantics: members[].role (entry-adapter / validator / side-effect-adapter) and edges[].edgeKind (data-flow / control-flow / validation). No role + edgeKind, no promotion.
  • Replacement declaration: replacement.legacyUris[] explicitly enumerates the legacy entry being replaced, e.g. legacy://app/checkout.py#L1-L1500.
  • Verifiable equivalence: a map-equivalence-report proves the map behaves like the legacy entry. Known divergences are allowed — but only if explicitly enumerated in knownDivergences[].
  • Safe exit: reaching legacy-retired requires a rollback-proof or retirement-proof. No reverse path, no retirement.

Five-stage replacement lifecycle

Legacy is never cut over in one step. It is a gated five-stage pipeline; every transition is guarded by police + evidence:

draft

Map created. No replacement claim yet.

shadow

Runs parallel to legacy. Compares, never decides.

canary

Small slice goes live. Rollback path preserved.

active

Becomes the entry. Legacy demoted to backup.

legacy-retired

Old entry retired. Rollback proof required.

shadow → canary needs a map-integration pass; canary → active needs equivalence + propagation + human review; active → retired needs a rollback proof. No stage can be skipped.

Three data tiers, one golden property

Everything around the map is classified into three tiers, each with its own tolerance rule:

  • Source-of-truth: atom source, map.spec.json, capsule manifest. Must be verifiable and rebuildable; never overwritten by cache.
  • Derived state: registry, Mermaid diagrams, health reports. Deletable and rebuildable; must carry generatedAt.
  • Local volatile: daemon PID, guide cache. Off-git by default; safe to delete on corruption.
Combined with the Rescue Police and Disaster Recovery CLI, this guarantees a golden property: as long as source-of-truth survives, every registry and derived report can be rebuilt from scratch. Even after a corrupted upgrade or a botched retirement, you still have a road back to a known-good map.

Why this matters for multi-agent refactors

Without the map layer, your five agents can each produce clean atoms — but the result is a pile of cells on the floor with no organ to transplant in. The legacy checkout.py still owns the entry point, the integration tests still target it, and nobody can say with confidence whether the new constellation matches the old behavior.

With the map, you have a single named thing that declares which legacy URIs it replaces, carries an equivalence report, walks through a gated lifecycle, and ships with a rollback proof before the old entry can be retired. Atoms answer "what is this piece of code?"; maps answer "how is this legacy being replaced, with what evidence, and how do we roll back if it goes wrong?"
